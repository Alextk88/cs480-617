# Boston Gentrification, Diversity, and Inequality

An interactive, scroll-based data story showing how gentrification in Boston’s low-income tracts increased racial diversity while affordability and economic inequality remained major issues.

Core message: **Diversity increased, but equality did not automatically improve. Housing became less affordable, and income and education gaps remain uneven across racial and ethnic groups.**

## What This Project Analyzes

This project combines historical gentrification data from 1990 to 2016 with 2024 American Community Survey data for Boston. The goal is to understand whether increased diversity in gentrified areas also means increased equality.

The story focuses on:

- Racial composition change in low-income gentrified tracts from 1990 to 2016
- 2024 Boston citywide racial context using ACS data
- Changes in median rent, median income, and median home value
- Housing affordability ratios over time
- Which racial and ethnic groups are closest to rent and home affordability benchmarks
- The relationship between bachelor’s degree attainment and mean income by race/ethnicity

## Main Argument

Low-income gentrified tracts became more racially diverse from 1990 to 2016. However, housing became less affordable relative to income during the same period. When 2024 Boston income data is compared against affordability benchmarks, White and White non-Hispanic residents are closest to the rent and home-value thresholds, while Black, Hispanic/Latino, Some other race, and Two or more races fall below both.

The project argues that increased diversity alone does not prove equality improved. Since bachelor’s degree attainment and income appear connected, improving access to higher education, career pathways, and economic opportunity for lower-income minority groups may be one possible way to reduce long-term inequality.

## Important Data Note

The 1990 and 2016 values come from low-income gentrified tract data. The 2024 race, income, and education values come from Boston citywide ACS data. The 2024 values are used as present-day context, not as a direct continuation of the same tract-level dataset.
