Boston gentrification created diversity, but affordability inequality grew.

Low-income gentrified tracts became more diverse from 1990 to 2016. But housing became less affordable, and 2024 Boston data shows income and education remain uneven across racial and ethnic groups.

Diversity

Diversity increased, but that does not mean equality improved.

Low-income gentrified tracts became more racially diverse from 1990 to 2016. The 2024 point is Boston citywide ACS context, not the same tract-level dataset.

Housing costs

Home values rose far beyond income, making ownership harder to reach.

From 1990 to 2016, median home value rose from $292,000 to $425,000, while median income rose from $59,900 to $75,080.

Affordability

Housing became less affordable relative to income.

The home value-to-income ratio increased from 4.87 to 5.66, while annual rent-to-income also rose.

Unequal pressure

The same rent or home value is not equally affordable across groups.

White and White non-Hispanic residents are closest to the affordability benchmarks, while Black, Hispanic/Latino, Some other race, and Two or more races fall below both thresholds.

Education and income

Education and income move together, but gaps remain.

Groups with higher bachelor’s degree attainment tend to have higher mean income, suggesting education is connected to economic opportunity.

Conclusion

Diversity increased, but equality requires affordability and opportunity.

The data suggests that gentrification in Boston’s low-income tracts coincided with greater racial diversity, but that diversity did not automatically create equal outcomes. Housing became less affordable relative to income, and 2024 ACS data shows that income and bachelor’s degree attainment remain uneven across racial and ethnic groups. Because education and income appear closely connected, improving access to higher education, career pathways, and economic opportunity for lower-income minority groups may be one potential way to reduce long-term inequality.
